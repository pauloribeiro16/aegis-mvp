#### Validated relationships (from CRDA-deep §D-03.1)

All 6 pairs verified by CRDA-deep as **SAME** (CRDA-deep corrected CRDA's
initial classifications — 2 pairs initially marked **DIFFERENT PERSPECTIVE**
and 4 pairs marked **COMPLEMENTARY** were all verified as SAME after
OJ-text analysis). The underlying obligation is the **same substantive
identity-management duty** (managing identities of principals that
interact with a system — for authentication, authorisation, verification
or accountability purposes); the pairs differ on the **identity subject**
that each regulation targets. The four regulators operate at four
**different identity boundaries**:

- **GDPR** — data subjects (external, rights-exercise context): the
  controller verifies the identity of a natural person making a request
  under Art. 15–22 (Art. 12(6)) and the data subject supplies enabling
  information (Art. 11(2)). Proportionate to risk; minimal collection
  per Art. 5(1)(c).
- **NIS 2** — workforce (internal, joiner-mover-leaver): the entity
  manages its own personnel identities throughout the employment
  lifecycle — provisioning on induction, entitlement review on role
  change, revocation on separation. Continuously; appropriate and
  proportionate per Art. 21(1).
- **CRA** — product users, services, and integrated components
  (product-side): the manufacturer manages identities and credentials
  for human users of the product, service identities, and identities of
  integrated components, in a "verifiable manner" throughout the
  operational lifetime. Unique product identification (type/batch/serial
  per Art. 13(15) + Annex II §3) backs the SBOM-correlated identity
  register.
- **DORA** — staff + service-to-service identities (financial-entity-side):
  the strong-authentication obligation of Art. 9(4)(d) covers
  financial-entity staff and machine/service identities with identity
  assertions managed, protected and conveyed under standards.

The identity objects are **different** but the identity-management duty
is the **same substantive obligation** — verified by CRDA-deep as
SAME after OJ-text analysis. There is no contradiction because no
clause forbids what another mandates; a single organisation across
all four regulators must operate a **multi-boundary identity
architecture**, with the boundaries explicitly documented. CRA's
"verifiable manner" (Annex I Part I (2)(d)) is **stricter** than GDPR's
"reasonable means proportionate to risk" (Art. 12(6)) — the manufacturer
must demonstrate that identities are managed verifiably, not merely that
verification is proportional.

**Objective.** Identities of principals that interact with the entity's
systems, products, services, components and data are managed in a
verifiable, lifecycle-aware manner with documented boundaries between
identity types and explicit hand-off protocols at boundary surfaces.

**Considerations.**
- All four participating regulations impose the same substantive identity-management duty but each targets a distinct identity subject at a distinct boundary and anchors to a distinct threshold: GDPR data-subject-side proportionate verification on reasonable doubt (Art. 12(6) + Art. 11(2); minimal collection per Art. 5(1)(c)); NIS 2 workforce-side continuous joiner-mover-leaver lifecycle under HR-security discipline (Art. 21(2)(i); the access-control policy and asset-management sub-domains are coordinated by the literal AND in the same sub-point); CRA product-side verifiable identity-management for users, services and integrated components (Annex I Part I (2)(d) + Art. 13(15) + Annex II §3; the "verifiable manner" reading is stricter than the GDPR proportionality test); DORA financial-entity-side strong-authentication identity assertion management (Art. 9(4)(d); the identity-lifecycle is one of the three coordinated controls in the same sub-point — strong authentication + key protection + encryption).
- The high-level SO describes the common identity-management duty (verifiable, lifecycle-aware, boundary-disciplined); the per-regulation perspective is preserved in the four sub-SOs (identity subject, threshold anchor, evidence template, recipient).
- A single organisation across all four regulators must operate a multi-boundary identity architecture with explicit boundary hand-offs: data-subject verification flows into staff authentication at the rights-exercise endpoint; staff identity feeds into product-user identity via deployment; product-user identity feeds back into DORA service-identity via integration.
- For parties that touch only one regulatory scope, only the triggered sub-SO applies.
- CRA is the strictest knowledge-test floor ("verifiable manner") and discharges the GDPR `reasonable means proportionate to risk` on the identity-management dimension when the same party operates both manufacturer-side and controller-side roles.
- DORA's identity-assertion discipline (PR.AA-04) — managing, protecting and conveying identity assertions in a manner that verifies authenticity — is the strictest assertion-conveyance floor and supports the NIS 2 `where appropriate` test on authentication strength for financial-sector entities also within retained NIS 2 scope.

#### Scope-overlap principle (operational). The high-level SO
(`SO-D-03.1.HL`) engages on a given party **if and only if ≥2 applicable
regulations' identity-boundary scope-determiners overlap on the same
party** (e.g. a controller that is also a NIS 2 essential entity
operating CRA-regulated products and running a DORA-class financial ICT
system — a vertically-integrated manufacturer-controller on a
financial-services stack with a multi-boundary identity architecture).
Pure co-applicability of disjoint-boundary regulations on different
identity objects does **not** trigger cross-regulation aggregation — see
the Activation logic in the file header. For a 5-FTE SaaS startup with
GDPR + CRA applicability but no NIS 2 / DORA applicability, only the
GDPR and CRA sub-SOs activate; the high-level SO engages on the same
party if both apply, with the boundary between data-subject verification
and product-user identity management documented as the controlled
hand-off. **The four identity boundaries are layered, not collapsed**:
data subjects are external (GDPR); workforce is internal (NIS 2);
product users + services + components are product-side (CRA); staff +
service identities are financial-entity-side (DORA). The high-level SO
surfaces the common identity-management duty but does **NOT** aggregate
the per-reg identity subjects, threshold anchors, or evidence templates.

## GDPR

##### Art. 5(1)(c) — Data minimisation

**Clause: GDPR-CL03** | type: principle | obligatedParty: CONTROLLER | obligationType: CONTINUOUS

**Berry anchor:** §3.3.5 (vagueness), §5.1 (`adequate`, `relevant`, `necessary`).

**Instance 1 — VAG / S3 / `adequate`, `relevant`, `necessary`**

Three inquiry-resistant adjectives; no measurement criterion. Art. 5(2) accountability shifts burden of demonstration to controller; Art. 35(7)(b) DPIA requires necessity assessment.

**Variant readings:**
| # | Reading | Disambiguation source |
|---|---|---|
| R1 | Adequate iff it serves the designed purpose; necessity presumed by default. | None in OJ; industry self-regulation. |
| R2 | Adequate iff proportionate to purpose; demonstrable ex ante. | Art. 5(2) accountability; Art. 35(7)(b) DPIA. |
| R3 | Adequate iff least-intrusive among equivalent means under Art. 52(1) Charter necessity test. | CJEU C-62/14 Gasket; Charter Art. 52(1). |

R1 vs R3 yield materially distinct compliance obligations (R1 permits default collection patterns; R3 requires per-field justification).

**Instance 2 — SCOPE-Q / S2 / `purposes` (plural)**

`purposes` is plural — the minimisation test applies to each purpose. Berry §5.2.1 (plural-scope `purposes`). S2 because Art. 5(2) accountability and Art. 35(7)(b) DPIA partially anchor the reading.

##### Art. 6(1)(a) — Consent

**Clause: GDPR-CL08** | type: lawfulness base | obligatedParty: CONTROLLER | obligationType: PER-PURPOSE (continuous)

#### Recurring patterns

- `necessary` appears in **6 of 26 clauses** — the dominant vague term of Chapter II (consistent with Berry's identification of `necessary` as one of the most-frequent vague terms in legal text).
- 6 lawfulness bases in Art. 6(1)(a)–(f) — each independently enumerated, each with `necessary` qualifier. The 6-fold enumeration creates a coordination question (any single basis, or all must hold?) — Berry §5.4.7 menu-card pattern.
- `explicit consent` (Art. 9(2)(a)) vs `specific consent` (Art. 4(11)) — POLY between `explicit` and `specific`. EDPB Guidelines attempt to fix but the OJ text does not.
- Art. 4(1) `identifiable` ambiguity propagates throughout the chapter (Art. 5(1)(e) `form which permits identification`, Art. 11 `additional information`).

#### Cross-article structural observations

1. **Art. 5 is the foundational principle layer; Art. 6 is the lawfulness-anchor layer.** Every downstream clause derives its binding force from one of these two anchors. Berry §5.5 parallelism: the 7 sub-principles of Art. 5 are structurally parallel and admit parallel interpretation issues.
2. **Art. 6(1) is a 6-fold disjunction.** The phrase `at least one of the following applies` (Art. 6(1) intro) anchors the inclusive-OR reading, but the cross-clause interplay (Art. 6(1)(f) final subparagraph excludes public authorities from (f); Art. 6(2) introduces Member State derogations for (c) and (e)) creates a layered structure.
3. **Art. 8 is incomplete.** The 16/13-age gating is fixed by Member State law per Art. 8(1); Art. 8(2) `reasonable efforts` is the operative controller obligation. The `available technology` qualifier shifts the standard over time.
4. **Art. 9 is the densest special-category provision.** The 10 sub-exceptions (a)–(j) of Art. 9(2) each open a distinct pathway for special-category processing. Several cross-reference Charter Art. 52(1) proportionality (the `essence of the right` phrasing).
5. **Art. 11 is under-utilised.** The identification-exemption applies only to Art. 5–9 obligations; data subject rights (Art. 15–20) are exempted only when the data subject cannot provide `additional information` for re-identification. Many controllers over-rely on Art. 11 to refuse SARs (the practical reading is narrower than the headline).

#### Anti-pattern notes for downstream files

- Chapter III (Rights, Art. 12–23) inherits Art. 5(1)(a) `transparent`, Art. 6 lawfulness bases, and Art. 7 consent conditions. Disambiguation chains should refer back to Chapter II anchors.
- Chapter IV (Controller/Processor, Art. 24–39) inherits Art. 5(2) accountability, Art. 6 lawfulness, Art. 7 demonstrability, Art. 8 child consent, Art. 9 special categories, Art. 10 criminal convictions. Art. 24's `appropriate technical and organisational measures` is structurally parallel to Art. 5(1)(f) and Art. 32(1).
- Chapter V (Transfers, Art. 44–49) inherits Art. 4(1) `identifiable`, Art. 5(1)(f) `appropriate security`, Art. 9 special categories. Transfer mechanisms reference Art. 6 lawfulness bases.

### 4. Cross-clause structural observations (Art. 6–8)

1. **Art. 6 is the essential-requirements gate.** The two-part structure (Part I for product properties; Part II for processes) mirrors GDPR's two-tier obligation (Art. 25 design + Art. 32 security). The installation-condition proviso (CL02) is CRA-unique and operationally consequential: a non-conforming product that is "properly installed" satisfies Art. 6(a).

2. **Art. 7's two criteria (CL06, CL07) define important products.** The criteria are disjunctive (criterion A OR B). Three materially distinct readings admit materially different compliance populations. The Commission's 11 December 2025 implementing acts (CL09) will refine the criteria.

3. **Art. 8's certification requirement (CL10) is the most consequential single clause in this article.** Critical products require European cybersecurity certification at assurance level "substantial." As of mid-2025, **no European cybersecurity certification scheme has been adopted under the CSA that covers critical-product categories.** The fallback (CL12) routes products to Art. 32(3) conformity assessment (modules B+C or H).

4. **The Art. 7/8/Alex III/IV architecture is a 3-tier classification** (analogous to AI Act's risk tiers):
   - Default (Art. 6): all products meet Annex I (essential requirements) — Annex I compliance route.
   - Important (Art. 7): Class I or Class II per Annex III — Art. 32(2)/(3) conformity assessment.
   - Critical (Art. 8): Annex IV — European cybersecurity certification at substantial level (or Art. 32(3) fallback).

   Each tier imposes progressively stricter conformity-assessment obligations.

5. **`core functionality` (CL04) is the single most consequential classification term.** The reading determines which products fall into Class I/II vs default. R1–R3 admit materially different compliance populations.

6. **Annex III/IV are flagged for Layer 2.** Their ambiguity is definitional regress ("core functionality of a product category") rather than Berry linguistic in the strict sense.
